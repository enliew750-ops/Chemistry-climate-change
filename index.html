01 · Introduction

Why a warming planet comes down to chemistry

People usually talk about climate change through weather and disaster: heatwaves, floods, melting ice. But the actual cause sits at a much smaller scale. Certain gases, shaped a certain way, absorb certain wavelengths of infrared light. That's really the whole mechanism, once you strip away the headlines. This project follows that idea from atoms up to atmosphere: what the chemistry of the greenhouse effect actually is, which human activities are pushing it further, what evidence shows the climate is changing, and a hands-on experiment that tries to model a small piece of it.

CORE QUESTION

How do greenhouse gases contribute to climate change, what human activities increase their concentrations, and how can their effects be reduced?

02 · The Greenhouse Effect

The Greenhouse Effect

A natural process, not a flaw

The greenhouse effect is what keeps Earth warm enough for life to exist at all. Certain gases in the atmosphere absorb thermal infrared radiation coming off Earth's surface, then re-emit it in random directions, including back down toward the surface. That slows how fast heat actually leaves the planet.NASA, 2024

NASA puts Earth's average surface temperature at around 15°C because of this. Without any greenhouse gases, it would sit closer to −18°C, too cold for liquid water to exist across most of the surface.NASA, 2024

The energy pathway

Solar radiation→ Earth's surface warms→ Thermal infrared emitted→ Greenhouse gases absorb→ Re-emitted in all directions→ Slower loss to space

Six steps, on repeat: sunlight enters, the surface absorbs it and warms, the surface releases infrared radiation, greenhouse gas molecules catch some of it, that energy gets re-emitted in different directions, and overall, heat leaves the planet more slowly than it would without those gases in the way.

The chemistry chain

Elements→ Covalent bonds→ Molecular structure→ Interaction with infrared radiation→ Greenhouse effect

Different shapes vibrate differently, and only some of those vibrations line up with infrared wavelengths. That's why greenhouse gases can't really be swapped for one another, and why even a gas present in tiny amounts, like methane or nitrous oxide, can still matter a lot.

04 · Human Activities

Human Activities That Increase Greenhouse Gases

Human activity releases CO₂, CH₄, and N₂O faster than natural processes can remove them, mostly through how we make energy, use land, grow food, manufacture things, and get rid of waste. Click a card to open it.

Coal mostly goes toward electricity and industry. Oil gets refined into petrol, diesel, and aviation fuel for transport. Natural gas, which is mostly methane, is used for heating, power, and industrial processes. Combustion follows the same basic pattern each time:

Fuel + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O + energy

The EPA lists fossil-fuel combustion for electricity, heat, and transport as the single largest source of human greenhouse-gas emissions.EPA, 2025

Cars, buses, trucks, ships, and aircraft mostly burn fossil fuels to release the energy they need to move. When combustion is complete, the carbon in the fuel is oxidised into carbon dioxide.

Fuel→ Combustion→ Energy release→ CO₂ emissions

Forests pull CO₂ out of the air through photosynthesis and lock the carbon into biomass:

6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

Clearing forests hits twice: fewer trees means less CO₂ removed going forward, and burning or decomposing the cleared vegetation releases carbon that was already stored.

Livestock digestion and manure management release methane. Flooded rice fields create oxygen-poor soil where microorganisms also produce methane. Nitrogen fertilisers add compounds to soil that get transformed into nitrous oxide through the nitrogen cycle.

Cement production is a good example of direct chemical emissions: heating limestone releases CO₂ as a product of the reaction itself, not just from whatever fuel was burned to heat it:

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

Meanwhile, organic waste breaks down anaerobically in the deeper, oxygen-poor layers of a landfill, and methane is one of the by-products.

05 · Climate Change

Global Warming & Climate Change

People use these two terms interchangeably a lot, but they aren't quite the same thing.

Global warming

The long-term rise in Earth's average surface temperature, driven by a shift in the balance between incoming solar energy and outgoing thermal energy.

Climate change

A wider term that covers long-term shifts across the whole climate system: temperature, rainfall, ocean conditions, ice cover, sea level, ecosystems. Global warming is one piece of that bigger picture.

How they connect

Human activities→ ↑ Greenhouse gases→ Enhanced greenhouse effect→ Global warming→ Changes across the climate system→ Climate change

NASA is direct about this: human activity, particularly the kind that raises greenhouse-gas levels, is the dominant reason for the warming seen since the mid-20th century.NASA, 2024

The Keeling Curve: CO₂ measured at Mauna Loa

Atmospheric CO₂ has been measured continuously at Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii since 1958, the longest direct record of its kind anywhere. Hover or tap a point for the value that year. Figures below are annual averages derived from NOAA's published growth-rate data.NOAA GML, 2025

Six independent lines of evidence

  • Temperature records: combined weather-station, ocean-buoy, and satellite data, checked against ice-core paleoclimate records.
  • Atmospheric CO₂: direct monitoring at Mauna Loa since 1958, plus ancient air trapped in Antarctic ice cores.
  • Glaciers & ice sheets: most of the world's glaciers have been retreating since the 1950s, and the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets are losing mass.IPCC, 2021
  • Sea level: tracked with tide gauges and satellite altimetry, rising from both thermal expansion and melting land ice.
  • Ecosystems & species: many species shifting toward the poles and to higher elevations as their suitable climate zones move.
  • Seasonal patterns: longer growing seasons, earlier flowering, shifted migration timing, shrinking Arctic sea ice.
07 · Impacts

Impacts of Climate Change

These effects ripple through connected systems rather than showing up one at a time. Melting ice raises sea level, warmer oceans disrupt marine life, shifting rainfall hits agriculture, and extreme heat puts pressure on human communities.IPCC, 2022

Oceans

Warmer water, more frequent marine heatwaves, coral bleaching, and acidification from dissolved CO₂:

CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃

Sea levels

Rising from thermal expansion of warming seawater plus meltwater from glaciers and ice sheets, which raises the risk of coastal flooding and erosion.

Ecosystems

Species shifting range, mismatched breeding and migration timing, and particular strain on coral reefs and polar ecosystems that have little room to adapt.

Agriculture

Heat and drought cut yields in some regions, heavy rainfall and flooding damage crops in others, and livestock face more heat stress overall.

Human communities

Extreme heat raises health risks, flooding damages infrastructure, and exposure isn't equal. Communities with fewer resources tend to face disproportionate risk.IPCC, 2022

Extreme weather

Warming shifts the conditions behind heatwaves, heavy rainfall, drought, tropical cyclones, and wildfire risk, without necessarily causing any one event on its own.

08 · Experiment & Results

Experiment & Results

Investigating the effect of carbon dioxide concentration on temperature, using a simple model of the greenhouse effect.

AIM

Test whether more CO₂ raises the temperature inside a closed container under a constant light source.

INDEPENDENT VARIABLE

Gas composition: normal air vs. CO₂-enriched air.

DEPENDENT VARIABLE

Temperature inside the container (°C).

CONTROLLED VARIABLES

Container, light source, distance, exposure time, starting temperature, gas volume.

HYPOTHESIS

If the concentration of carbon dioxide inside a container goes up, the container should show a bigger temperature increase than one with normal air, because CO₂ is a greenhouse gas that absorbs and re-emits thermal infrared radiation.

Producing the CO₂

Carbon dioxide comes from reacting sodium hydrogencarbonate (baking soda) with dilute ethanoic acid (vinegar):

NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂

Method

Set up two identical transparent containers with temperature sensors placed the same way in each.
Keep normal air in the control container, and add CO₂-enriched air to the experimental one using the vinegar and baking soda reaction.
Make sure both containers start at the same temperature before sealing them.
Place both containers the same distance from a lamp and switch it on at the same time.
Record temperature at regular intervals (every 2 minutes, over 14 minutes).
Repeat when there's time, since more trials mean more reliable results.

Safety

  • Safety goggles and lab coats worn the whole time.
  • Never seal the vinegar and baking soda reaction inside a rigid container, since it produces gas.
  • Avoid breathing in concentrated CO₂; work somewhere ventilated.
  • Handle the lamp carefully, it gets hot during the experiment.
STATUS

Results below are from a single completed trial. As the paper's own evaluation notes, one trial isn't enough to establish a strong pattern, repeating each condition and averaging would make the results more reliable.

Chemistry interpretation

Over 15 minutes, the normal-air bag warmed by 2°C while the CO₂-enriched bag warmed by 5°C, more than double the temperature rise. That supports the hypothesis: with more CO₂ molecules available inside the bag, more of the outgoing infrared radiation gets absorbed and re-emitted back inward instead of escaping, so the bag loses heat less efficiently and its temperature climbs faster.

CO₂ ↑→ More IR absorbed→ More IR re-emitted inside container→ Slower heat loss→ Temperature rise

Limitations of the model

  • Doesn't reproduce Earth's atmosphere, circulation, clouds, or oceans.
  • The lamp might transfer heat directly rather than purely through re-radiated infrared energy.
  • CO₂ concentration inside the container isn't measured precisely.
  • One trial alone isn't statistically reliable; repeating and averaging improves confidence.

This experiment can show a simplified relationship under controlled conditions, but it can't prove human-caused climate change by itself. Its results should be read next to the wider evidence in Section 06, not as standalone proof.

09 · Strategies & Conclusion

Strategies & Conclusion

Cutting emissions takes coordinated change across energy, transport, forests, agriculture, and industry. No single strategy gets it done alone.IPCC, 2022

Energy

  • Solar, wind, hydro, and geothermal replace fossil combustion
  • Energy efficiency lowers demand everywhere
  • Needs storage and grid upgrades to handle variability

Transport

  • Public transport cuts emissions per passenger
  • Electric vehicles have no tailpipe CO₂
  • How clean it really is depends on the electricity source

Forests

  • Stopping deforestation protects existing carbon stores
  • Reforestation pulls CO₂ out as trees grow
  • Carbon storage takes years to build up

Agriculture

  • Better livestock and manure management cuts CH₄
  • More precise fertiliser use cuts N₂O
  • Has to balance emissions cuts with feeding people

Industry

  • Electrification and low-emission energy sources
  • Efficiency gains in heating and machinery
  • Carbon capture for emissions that are hard to avoid
OVERALL

No single fix

The IPCC finds mitigation options across every major sector, many of them ready to deploy soon, but how well they work depends on scale, speed, funding, and whether they actually get coordinated.IPCC, 2022

Conclusion

Greenhouse gases warm the planet because of their molecular structure. Their covalent bonds and shapes let them absorb and re-emit infrared radiation. Human activity has clearly raised the concentration of CO₂, CH₄, and N₂O since industrialisation began, turning a natural process into one that's now shifting Earth's energy balance. Independent evidence, temperature records, atmospheric CO₂ measurements, glacier retreat, sea-level rise, and ecosystem shifts, all point to the same conclusion: the climate is changing, and human greenhouse-gas emissions are the main reason since the mid-20th century.IPCC, 2021

The experiment in Section 08 tests one small piece of that chain directly: whether raising CO₂ concentration in a sealed, lit container produces a measurable temperature increase. It does, the CO₂-enriched bag warmed more than twice as much as the normal-air control over the same 15 minutes. Read alongside the broader evidence above, that result isn't standalone proof, but it is a hands-on look at a mechanism that plays out at planetary scale.
